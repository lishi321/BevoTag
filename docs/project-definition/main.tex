\section{Introduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This Project Definition states the design problem that the BevoTag team will solve in Senior
Design and explains how the five-member team has organized itself to solve it. BevoTag is a small
tag that a student or staff member attaches to a laptop, bag, or piece of university equipment to
locate it anywhere on the University of Texas at Austin campus. Global Positioning System (GPS)
signals do not penetrate well indoors \cite[Sec.~I]{hechan}, and consumer tags such as the Apple
AirTag update an item's location only when a nearby phone from the same manufacturer detects them
\cite{airtag}. BevoTag is an open tag because it needs no manufacturer's phone network: it
estimates the item's position from campus Wi-Fi and its own motion sensor and reports that position
to its owner on a campus map. The team will build a tag, firmware, a building survey, and an
application that together place an item in the correct room in more than 90\% of trials. Two
sub-teams under a Project Lead will do this work; the team has chosen its technical approach and
will next build a data logger for its first Wi-Fi and motion measurements.

The design problem is to track an item continuously, indoors and outdoors, without new
infrastructure. Wi-Fi measurements alone can mislocate a device by several meters because signals
fluctuate as the environment changes \cite{hechan}, \cite[Table~4]{rssi}. A low-cost motion sensor
alone drifts even faster, by more than 150~m within 60~s \cite[Sec.~6.3]{imu}. Combining the two
lets each correct the other. The project will test this approach in surveyed areas beginning with
the Engineering Education and Research Center (EER).

The design must meet measurable targets within limits that standards, ethics, and team policies
set. Beyond room identification, the system must keep its median position error under 5~m, update
an item's location at least every 10~s while the item moves, and run at least 24~hours on one
battery charge. Radio, data-security, and battery-safety standards and university network policy
constrain the design, and the team commits to recording no one's network traffic, to encrypting and
limiting location data, and to using the campus network only as an ordinary client. Esteban
Martinez and Li Shi will build the tag hardware and firmware, Andrew Yin and Abhirit Das will build
the localization software and the user application, and Ethan Chua will coordinate the work as
Project Lead.

\section{Problem Statement}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BevoTag must locate a tagged item to within a few meters across indoor and outdoor areas of campus,
using only existing campus Wi-Fi and its own motion sensor. Buildings block and reflect satellite
signals \cite{yoder}, and low-cost motion sensors drift \cite{imu}, so no single source of position
is reliable on its own. Fusing Wi-Fi fingerprints, the signal patterns recorded at known locations,
with motion sensing is the most promising approach, and a successful design must meet measurable
targets for room accuracy, update rate, and battery life.

\subsection{Design Problem Description}
A successful BevoTag design must estimate an item's location indoors and outdoors without
infrastructure the team cannot control, and it must show that location to the item's owner on a
map. Existing trackers fail this need because satellite signals do not reach indoors and
crowdsourced tags depend on passing phones. BevoTag addresses the need by fusing Wi-Fi fingerprints
with motion sensing, an approach that brings three main challenges: computing the position within
the tag's limited power, capturing CSI from radio signals that change from moment to moment, and
correcting sensor drift. Because the tag reports
location, the design must also satisfy radio, data-security, and battery-safety standards and
protect the privacy of bystanders and owners.

\subsubsection{The Need for On-Campus Item Tracking}
Students, faculty, and staff need a way to find misplaced items anywhere on the main UT Austin
campus, known as the Forty Acres. Laptops, bags, and shared university equipment move between
classrooms, labs, libraries, and outdoor plazas many times a day, and an item left behind can be
anywhere among dozens of multistory buildings. A useful tracker must therefore work indoors, on any
floor, and in the narrow outdoor spaces between tall buildings. The team's goal is tracking that
needs neither new infrastructure, such as beacons installed in every building, nor a passerby who
owns a particular brand of smartphone.

\subsubsection{Why Existing Solutions Fall Short}
Each of the three existing approaches to tracking fails on campus. Satellite positioning cannot
reach indoors, crowdsourced Bluetooth tags stop updating when no compatible phone passes by, and
Wi-Fi positioning that relies on signal strength alone often errs by enough to place an item in the
wrong room.

Satellite positioning fails indoors and degrades between tall buildings. Global Navigation
Satellite Systems (GNSS), the family of satellite positioning systems that includes GPS, compute a
receiver's position from faint signals broadcast by satellites in medium Earth orbit; GPS
satellites, for example, orbit about 20,200~km above the Earth \cite{gps}. These signals do not
penetrate well indoors \cite[Sec.~I]{hechan}. Between tall buildings, signal blockage and multipath,
the arrival of signals only after they reflect off nearby walls, degrade GNSS so much that one 2009
experiment with a precise receiver lacking inertial aiding measured root-mean-square position errors
greater than 9~m \cite[Sec.~1.1.1]{yoder}.

Crowdsourced tags locate an item only when a compatible phone happens to pass nearby. The Apple
AirTag and the Samsung Galaxy SmartTag2 broadcast a short-range Bluetooth signal, and nearby phones
from the same manufacturer detect that signal and report its location \cite{airtag},
\cite{smarttag}. In an empty lab at night no such phone passes by, so the tag's location stops
updating until one does.

Wi-Fi positioning that relies only on signal strength is too coarse for reliable room-level
tracking. These systems estimate position from the received signal strength indicator (RSSI), a
measure of how strong each nearby Wi-Fi signal is when it reaches the device. Signal strength
fluctuates as people move and the environment changes \cite[Sec.~III-B]{hechan}, so RSSI
fingerprinting typically locates a device only to within 1 to 5~m \cite[Table~4]{rssi}, which can
place it in the wrong room.

\subsubsection{Theory of Operation}
BevoTag combines two sources of position information whose errors differ: campus Wi-Fi
measurements, which are noisy but do not drift, and a motion sensor, which is smooth over short
periods but drifts. The final design may
compute this estimate on the tag or on a server; the team will decide after measuring the
prototype hardware, as the Design Challenges explain.

The system locates the tag by matching its Wi-Fi measurements against a survey of the surrounding building.
In addition to RSSI, some Wi-Fi chips report channel state information
(CSI), the frequency response of each of dozens of narrow sub-carriers \cite{espcsi}, which shows
how much the path weakens and delays the signal on each sub-carrier. Walls, furniture, and room
geometry shape these values, so CSI acts as a detailed fingerprint of a location \cite{yangcsi}.
The team will first survey a building by recording Wi-Fi measurements at many points whose
positions it knows. The system can then estimate the tag's position by finding the survey points
whose measurements best match the tag's current measurements.

A motion sensor tracks the tag between Wi-Fi fixes but drifts when it runs alone. An inertial
measurement unit (IMU), similar to the sensor that rotates a smartphone's screen, measures
acceleration along three axes and rotation about them. Adding up these measurements from a known
starting point, a technique called dead reckoning, tracks the tag's movement many times per
second. Small sensor errors accumulate, however, so the estimate drifts farther from the truth the
longer it runs: a simple low-cost inertial system can accumulate more than 150~m of average
position error within 60~s \cite[Sec.~6.3]{imu}.

A fusion algorithm combines the two sources so that the result neither jumps like Wi-Fi alone nor
drifts like the IMU alone. A standard candidate is the extended Kalman filter \cite{welch},
which uses the IMU to predict how the tag has moved and uses each new Wi-Fi fix to correct the
accumulated drift, weighting each source by how reliable it currently is. Outdoors, where
satellite signals are available, the same algorithm can also accept a GNSS position as an optional
third source.

\subsubsection{Design Functionality and Scope}
BevoTag is a system of a tag, a location server, and a user application that together turn Wi-Fi
and motion measurements into a position on a campus map. The project covers a working prototype
that the team will test in surveyed areas, starting with EER. It excludes cellular connectivity, a
campus-wide survey, mass-production tooling, and the tracking of people.

Any BevoTag design must measure, transmit, estimate, and display, as
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}\vpageref[]{fig:overview} shows. The tag measures nearby Wi-Fi signals and
its own motion (step~1) and sends these measurements over the campus Wi-Fi network to the location
server (step~2). The server combines them with a previously recorded survey of the building to
estimate the tag's position, or it receives an estimate that the tag computed itself (step~3). The
user application then displays that position on a campus map (step~4). To deliver this behavior,
any BevoTag design must perform four functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wi-Fi measurement:} record the RSSI and, where the hardware supports it, the CSI
    of signals from nearby campus access points in the 2.4~GHz band.
  \item \textbf{Motion tracking:} sample the IMU's acceleration and rotation fast enough to follow a
    walking person carrying the tag.
  \item \textbf{Position estimation:} fuse the motion data with Wi-Fi position fixes, correcting the
    drift that accumulates in the motion estimate.
  \item \textbf{Reporting:} send the latest position and device status to an application that the
    owner can view.
\end{itemize}

Within that scope, the prototype will use available parts and start with one building. The tag
will combine a Wi-Fi chip that can report CSI, such as the
ESP32-S3 \cite{espcsi}, with a low-noise IMU on a development board or a custom circuit board. Its
firmware will read the sensors and either process the measurements or send them to the server. The
survey will begin with EER and its adjacent outdoor plazas, and the application will display the
tag's position on a campus map.

Four related capabilities fall outside the project's scope. The tag will not use a cellular
Internet of Things connection, such as Long-Term Evolution for Machines (LTE-M) or Narrowband
Internet of Things (NB-IoT), because these require a paid data plan and
a second radio that would raise cost and power consumption, and campus Wi-Fi serves the target
areas; the survey will confirm that coverage. The team will not survey every building on campus,
because a survey requires walking each floor and recording measurements at many points. It will
instead test in a few defined benchmark areas and document the survey method so that others can
add buildings later. The team will 3D-print or buy the prototype enclosure rather than design it
for injection molding, since the goal is a working proof of concept rather than a manufacturable
product. Finally, BevoTag locates objects, not people, and the design excludes features that would
follow a person, such as continuous covert tracking.

\subsubsection{Design Challenges}
Three challenges will most strongly shape the design: deciding where to compute the position,
capturing CSI from radio signals that never repeat exactly, and correcting motion-sensor drift
quickly enough. Each challenge requires a measurement on prototype hardware before the team can
finalize the design.

The first challenge is deciding whether to compute the position on the tag or on the server.
Processing CSI and running a fusion filter on a small microcontroller strain its memory and
battery. Sending raw measurements to the server moves that work off the tag but keeps the radio on
longer, which also drains the battery. The team will settle this trade-off by measuring CSI capture
rates, IMU data rates, and power draw on the prototype.

The second challenge is capturing reliable CSI from signals that vary from moment to moment. A
receiver derives CSI from the training fields of frames that use orthogonal frequency-division
multiplexing (OFDM) \cite{espcsi}, \cite{std80211}, so the tag must capture it from OFDM frames that nearby access
points send, not from every frame it hears. The measurements recorded at a given spot also
fluctuate with noise and with changes in the environment, such as people walking through hallways
\cite[Sec.~III-B]{hechan}. The team will therefore record survey measurements at several times of
day and test the matching method on data that it did not use to build the survey.

The third challenge is correcting IMU drift before it grows large. Because the drift described in
the Theory of Operation grows quickly, Wi-Fi fixes must arrive often enough, and be reliable enough, to keep the fused estimate from diverging from the true
position. This requirement ties the Wi-Fi update rate to the accuracy targets in the Performance
Specifications.

\subsubsection{Relevant Standards}
Seven standards and policies constrain how BevoTag uses the radio spectrum, connects its sensors,
protects its data, powers itself, and uses the campus network. Their most direct effects are that
the tag must use a Wi-Fi chip that exposes CSI and a pre-certified radio module, keep its sensor
drivers separate from its estimation code, encrypt its telemetry, use a certified and protected
lithium battery, and act as an ordinary network client. Appendix~A lists the specific clauses of
each standard, how each affects the design, and how the team will verify compliance.

IEEE 802.11 \cite{std80211} defines the training fields from which BevoTag derives CSI and governs
how the tag joins campus networks. The standard specifies how Wi-Fi devices format and exchange
signals, including the training fields at the start of every OFDM frame, and the tag needs a chip
that reports CSI from those fields \cite{espcsi}.

Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rules in Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations
(C.F.R.), Part 15 \cite{fcc15}, lead the team to use a Wi-Fi module that already holds FCC
approval. Part 15 limits both the intended Wi-Fi transmissions and the unintended electrical noise
that the tag's processor and circuits emit, so that the tag does not interfere with campus
communications. Section 15.212 lets a radio module receive its own modular approval and, when the
installed module's FCC identifier (FCC ID) is not visible, requires the product to carry an outside
label such as ``Contains FCC ID'' that refers to the module \cite[\S15.212]{fcc15}. Such a module is
therefore the most practical way to meet these limits.

IEEE 1451.0 \cite{std1451}, the base standard of the IEEE 1451 smart transducer family, leads the
team to separate sensor drivers from the position-estimation code. The standard describes how
sensors should present their data and calibration information to a processor. Following its
approach lets the team replace either sensor without rewriting the fusion algorithm.

National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication 800-88
\cite{nist80088} and Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) 140-3 \cite{fips1403} determine
how the tag encrypts its reports and how a factory reset erases its memory. These federal
guidelines cover the erasure of stored data and the cryptographic methods that protect data, and
they apply because the tag stores network credentials and location history.

International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standard 62133-2 \cite{iec62133} sets the safety
requirements for the rechargeable lithium battery that powers the tag. Its tests address
overcharge, short circuit, and overheating, so the team will choose a certified cell and add a
protection circuit on its own board.

The university's acceptable use policy (AUP) \cite{utaup} governs how the tag may use the campus
network. It forbids deliberately scanning or probing any information resource without
authorization and interfering with other users' activities, which reinforces the ethical
commitments below.

\subsubsection{Ethical Considerations}
Because BevoTag's purpose is to report location, it creates obligations to people beyond its
owner. The team commits to five measures: recording only the signal measurements needed for
positioning, protecting stored location data, never disrupting the campus network, protecting
users from battery hazards and electronic waste, and honoring the licenses of the software and map
data the project uses.

The firmware will record only signal measurements and packet header fields, never the content of
anyone's network traffic. To capture CSI, the tag listens to Wi-Fi traffic that may come from other
people's devices. It will discard any frame whose transmitter is not an access point, so it never
stores another person's device address. From access point frames it will keep only the fields
needed for positioning, such as the access point address and signal strength, and it will not
decode, store, or transmit the contents of any packet.

The team will treat location history as personal information, because a history of where an item
has been reveals where its owner has been. The team will encrypt data in transit between the tag
and the server and restrict server access to the authenticated owner of each tag. It will also
delete stored location history at the end of the project and ensure that a factory reset erases
stored credentials and history, consistent with the guidelines in Appendix~A.

The tag will use the campus network only as an ordinary client. It will listen passively or
connect through networks, such as utexas-iot, that the university provides for devices of this
kind \cite{utiot}. It will never transmit traffic designed to disrupt, congest, or probe the
security of UT Austin's network.

The design will protect people from battery hazards and limit electronic waste. The tag carries a
small lithium battery near people and their belongings, so it will include protection against
overcharging, short circuits, and overheating. To limit waste, the team will favor a rechargeable
battery over disposable cells and will document how to dispose of the prototype's battery properly.

The team will respect the intellectual property of others. The firmware, server, and application
will build on third-party libraries and map data whose licenses may require attribution or limit
redistribution. The team will record the license of each component it uses, comply with those
terms, and cite the published research behind its methods in every project document.

\subsubsection{Project Deliverables}
At the end of EE~464, the team will deliver to Dr.\ Vikalo an integrated prototype that estimates
and displays the location of a tagged item within the surveyed campus areas, together with the
data and documentation needed to extend it. The delivery will consist of six items:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BevoTag hardware device:} a compact, battery-powered tag in an enclosure that
    collects Wi-Fi and motion measurements and sends them to the location server.
  \item \textbf{Embedded firmware:} software on the tag that reads the sensors, timestamps each
    measurement, monitors device status, and communicates over Wi-Fi.
  \item \textbf{Campus survey and map:} Wi-Fi survey data and a digital 3D map of the designated
    test areas that together let the system estimate, display, and evaluate the tag's location.
  \item \textbf{Location server software:} software that receives data from each tag, produces or
    stores its position estimate, and provides the result to the user application.
  \item \textbf{User application:} a web or mobile application in which a user can view a tag's
    estimated location and status on the campus map.
  \item \textbf{Documentation:} the source code, the survey procedure, and test results measured
    against the specifications in this report.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Requirements Specification}
A successful BevoTag implementation must place a tagged item in the correct room in more than 90\%
of trials, keep its median position error under 5~m, update the item's location at least every
10~s while the item moves, and run at least 24~hours on one battery charge. The tables in this
subsection set these and the other measurable targets for the system's inputs and outputs, user
interface, operating environment, and performance, without prescribing an implementation. The team
set these targets from published Wi-Fi positioning results \cite{rssi} and its own judgment of what
makes a tracker useful, and it will confirm them with Dr.\ Vikalo before detailed design.

\subsubsection{Input and Output Specifications}
The BevoTag system has three components (the tag, a location server, and the user application), and
its primary inputs are Wi-Fi signals and the tag's own motion. Its outputs are the tag's estimated
position, the time of that estimate, and the tag's status, which the application shows to the
user. Figure~\ref{fig:io}\vpageref[]{fig:io} diagrams these connections, and the specification
table after the diagram sets their values, chiefly a sensitivity of $-100$~dBm, a report at least
every 10~s while moving, and a display refresh within 1~s.

The diagram shows that every live measurement enters through the tag and every output leaves
through the application. The tag measures Wi-Fi signals, motion, and, optionally, GNSS signals, and it
sends these measurements and its status to the server as telemetry. The server holds the building
survey and map, which the team records in advance, produces or receives the position estimate, and exchanges location, status, and tag
selections with the application. A USB-C port charges the tag's battery and lets a computer running
the application configure and debug the tag.

\subsection{Team Policies}
The team's policies set how members communicate, what they owe one another, and how the team
decides questions and resolves conflict. Members use Discord for daily communication and GitHub for
code and task tracking, attend two team meetings each week and a mentor meeting every two weeks,
and meet internal deadlines set ahead of course deadlines. They review one another's work, decide
significant questions by majority vote after research, and settle technical disagreements with
data.

\subsubsection{Project Management Tools}
The team coordinates through four shared platforms. Discord is the primary communication channel,
with separate channels for general coordination, each sub-team, and meeting logistics so that
discussions stay organized and searchable. A shared Google Calendar holds meeting times, course
deadlines, and internal milestones. A shared GitHub repository keeps firmware, application code,
data-processing scripts, and circuit board and enclosure designs under version control, and GitHub
Issues and a GitHub Projects board record each task's owner, due date, and status. A shared Google
Drive folder stores reports, meeting notes, and survey data. The Project Lead handles email with
Dr.\ Vikalo and the Technical TA and copies the full team, so every member receives the same
guidance.

\subsubsection{Team Expectations}
Members respond promptly, come to meetings prepared, review one another's work, and finish work
early. The Conflict Resolution policy states how the team responds
when a member does not meet these expectations.

Members answer direct questions on Discord within 24~hours on weekdays and come to every meeting
prepared. They flag time-sensitive issues, such as a blocked task or an
approaching deadline, by mentioning the affected members directly. The team meets twice a week and
meets with Dr.\ Vikalo every two weeks. Members review the agenda before each meeting, notify the
team on Discord at least 24~hours before an unavoidable absence, and read the notes and follow up on
their action items after any meeting they miss. At the start of each meeting, members report what
they completed, what they plan to do next, and any blockers, and the Project Lead records these
updates and keeps the Projects board current.

Every piece of work passes a second member's review before it leaves its author. The reviewer
checks each pull request before the team merges it, each circuit board design before the team
orders boards, and each written section against the ECE Style Guide before submission.

Internal deadlines fall at least 48~hours before each course deadline so that the team has time to
integrate, review, and revise its work. Members who expect to miss an internal deadline tell the
team at least 48~hours in advance, so that the team can re-scope or redistribute the work.

\subsubsection{Conflict Resolution}
The team makes significant decisions by majority vote after research, settles technical disputes
with tests, mediates interpersonal conflicts, and answers missed responsibilities in escalating
steps. Unresolved technical risks go to Dr.\ Vikalo or the Technical TA, and unresolved conflicts go
to the Technical TA or the course instructor.

Significant decisions, such as selecting a positioning approach, choosing components, or changing
the project scope, follow a set procedure. The members most familiar with the question present the trade-offs of each option, supported by
datasheets, published results, cost estimates, or the team's own measurements. The team then
votes, and the Project Lead records the outcome and its rationale in the meeting notes. Each sub-team makes decisions that
affect only its own work and reports them at the next meeting. When the two members of a sub-team
disagree, the Project Lead breaks the tie.

The team settles technical disagreements with data. Where practical, it runs a quick test or trade
study against the performance specifications in Table~\ref{tab:perf} before voting. It brings any
unresolved disagreement that poses significant risk to the project to Dr.\ Vikalo or the Technical
TA.

Members first address interpersonal conflicts privately with one another. If that conversation
does not resolve the issue, an uninvolved member mediates. The team escalates any conflict that it
cannot resolve internally to the Technical TA or the course instructor.

The team responds to missed responsibilities in escalating steps. The Project Lead first raises a
missed commitment privately with the member to understand the cause and agree on a recovery plan.
The team discusses repeated missed deadlines or meetings at a team meeting and records the
corrective actions in the notes. If the problem continues, the team notifies the Technical TA and
the course instructor and reports each member's contributions accurately in peer evaluations.

\section{Conclusion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This Project Definition establishes what the BevoTag team must build and how the team will work
together to build it. Because satellite positioning fails inside buildings and existing tags depend
on other people's phones, the team will build a low-cost tag that locates an item on campus by
matching Wi-Fi measurements against a building survey and fusing them with motion-sensor data. The
design must place the item in the correct room in more than 90\% of trials, within the limits that
radio, data-security, and battery standards and the team's ethical commitments set. Two sub-teams
under a Project Lead will do the work under shared policies for communication, review, and
decisions. A working prototype in a few surveyed buildings is realistic, and the team's next steps
are a data logger, confirmed targets, and a survey of EER.

A working prototype within a few surveyed buildings is a realistic outcome despite the three
challenges that the Design Challenges describe. Computing the position may strain the tag's power,
CSI capture may prove unreliable as signals change, and drift may outpace Wi-Fi fixes. If CSI
capture fails, signal-strength fingerprinting fused with motion data remains a fallback at reduced
accuracy, and the team's experience in embedded systems, positioning research, and software
development covers the rest of the work. Success would matter beyond lost laptops: indoor
location-based services have social and commercial value \cite[Sec.~I]{hechan}, and on campus the
same tag could track shared lab equipment that moves among rooms.

Next, the team will confirm the specification targets with Dr.\ Vikalo, complete a data logger for
initial Wi-Fi and IMU measurements, and survey EER as the first test building. It will then refine
the system block diagram and component selection, and each member will plan an individual risk
reduction activity in that member's area of the design.
