\section{Introduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This Project Definition documents our team's understanding of the BevoTag design problem and
describes how the team has organized itself to solve it. BevoTag is a small tracking tag that a
student or staff member can attach to a laptop, bag, or piece of university equipment to locate it
anywhere on the University of Texas at Austin campus. Consumer tags such as the Apple AirTag
locate an item only when a nearby phone from the same manufacturer happens to detect it
\cite{airtag}, and satellite positioning such as GPS fails inside buildings. BevoTag instead
determines its own position from the Wi-Fi networks already installed across campus and from a
built-in motion sensor, then reports that position to a map-based application.

The report first explains the problem in plain terms: why existing trackers and satellite
positioning fall short on campus, how combining Wi-Fi signal measurements with motion sensing
can provide a continuous location estimate, what the tag will and will not do, and the standards
and ethical obligations, particularly location privacy, that shape any solution. It then translates
these needs into measurable requirements, including a median position error under 5~m and
correct room identification in more than 90\% of trials within surveyed buildings. Finally, the
report assigns the five team members to a hardware and firmware sub-team and a localization and
software sub-team under a single project lead, and it sets the policies the team will use to
communicate, track work, and resolve disagreements.

\section{Problem Statement}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Locating a small object reliably across a dense urban campus is difficult because radio signals
reflect off and are blocked by buildings, and low-cost motion sensors drift over time \cite{yoder}.
This section first describes the design problem, including the need that motivates BevoTag, the
technical approach it relies on, its functions and scope, and the standards and ethical issues that
constrain it. It then defines the inputs, outputs, interfaces, operating conditions, and performance
targets that a successful design must meet.

\subsection{Design Problem Description}
A successful BevoTag design must estimate the location of a tagged item indoors and outdoors,
without relying on infrastructure the team cannot control, and must present that location to its
owner in a form that is easy to interpret. The following subsections explain why this need exists,
why current products and satellite positioning do not meet it, the principle on which BevoTag
operates, and the scope, challenges, standards, ethical considerations, and deliverables that
follow from it.

\subsubsection{The Need for On-Campus Item Tracking}
Students, faculty, and staff routinely lose valuable items across the 40 Acres. Laptops, bags, and
shared university equipment move between classrooms, labs, libraries, and outdoor plazas many
times a day, and an item left behind can be anywhere among dozens of multi-story buildings. A
tracking tag on campus must therefore work indoors, on any floor, and in the narrow outdoor
spaces between tall buildings.

BevoTag serves UT Austin students, faculty, and employees who need to track personal
belongings or university equipment across campus. The team's motivation is to provide tracking
that requires neither new infrastructure, such as beacons installed in every building, nor a
passerby who owns a particular brand of smartphone.

\subsubsection{Why Satellite Positioning Fails on Campus}
Most outdoor tracking relies on Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), the family of
satellite positioning systems that includes GPS. A GNSS receiver computes its position from
faint signals broadcast by satellites more than 20,000~km away. Walls and roofs block those
signals almost entirely, so GNSS stops working inside buildings. Between tall buildings, the
signals that do arrive often reach the receiver only after reflecting off nearby walls, a
phenomenon called multipath, which corrupts the position estimate by tens of meters \cite{yoder}.
GNSS receivers also consume considerable power while searching for satellites, which shortens
battery life in a small tag.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Existing Tracking Solutions}
Current products avoid some of these problems but introduce others. Crowdsourced tags such as
the Apple AirTag and Samsung SmartTag broadcast a short-range Bluetooth signal and depend on
nearby phones from the same manufacturer to detect that signal and report its location
\cite{airtag}. When no such phone passes by, as in an empty lab at night, the tag's location stops
updating, and the system offers no access to its data for other applications. Dedicated GNSS
trackers suffer the indoor signal loss and power drain described above.

A third approach estimates position from the received signal strength indicator (RSSI), a measure
of how strong each nearby Wi-Fi signal is when it reaches the device. Because signal strength
changes as people walk by, doors open, and signals reflect off walls, RSSI-only systems commonly
mislocate a device by 5 to 15~m \cite{jiang}, which is often enough to place it in the wrong room.

\subsubsection{How BevoTag Determines Its Location}
BevoTag combines two complementary sources of information, neither of which is sufficient on its
own. The first is the campus Wi-Fi network. UT Austin operates Wi-Fi access points throughout
its buildings and plazas, including the \texttt{utexas} and \texttt{utexas-iot} networks
\cite{utiot}. In addition to RSSI, some Wi-Fi chips can report channel state information (CSI),
which describes how a signal was weakened and delayed on each of dozens of narrow frequency
sub-channels. Because walls, furniture, and room geometry shape these values, CSI acts as a
detailed fingerprint of a location. By first surveying a building, which means recording Wi-Fi
measurements at many points whose positions are known, the system can later estimate the tag's
position by matching its current measurements to the survey.

The second source is an inertial measurement unit (IMU), a motion sensor similar to the one that
rotates a smartphone's screen. A six-degree-of-freedom IMU measures acceleration along and
rotation about three axes. Adding up these measurements from a known starting point, a technique
called dead reckoning, tracks the tag's movement many times per second, but small sensor errors
accumulate, so the estimate drifts further from the truth the longer it runs \cite{mukherjee}. An
extended Kalman filter (EKF), a standard algorithm for combining noisy measurements, merges
the two sources: it uses the IMU to predict how the tag has moved and uses each new Wi-Fi
position fix to correct the accumulated drift, weighting each source by how reliable it currently
is. The result is a position estimate that is smoother than Wi-Fi alone and does not drift like the
IMU alone.

\subsubsection{Design Functionality and Scope}
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows how the parts of the BevoTag system work together. The tag
measures nearby Wi-Fi signals and its own motion (step 1) and sends these measurements over
the campus Wi-Fi network (step 2) to a server. The server compares the Wi-Fi measurements with
a previously recorded survey of the building and fuses them with the motion data to estimate the
tag's position (step 3). The user application then displays that position on a campus map
(step 4).

To deliver this behavior, any BevoTag design must perform four functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wi-Fi measurement:} record the RSSI and, where the hardware supports it, the CSI
    of signals from nearby campus access points in the 2.4~GHz band.
  \item \textbf{Motion tracking:} sample the IMU's acceleration and rotation fast enough to follow a
    walking person carrying the tag.
  \item \textbf{Position estimation:} fuse the motion data with Wi-Fi position fixes, correcting the
    drift that accumulates in the motion estimate.
  \item \textbf{Reporting:} send the latest position and device status to an application that the
    owner can view.
\end{itemize}

The project includes a working tag built on a developer board or custom circuit board that
combines a Wi-Fi chip able to capture CSI, such as the ESP32-S3, with a low-noise IMU. It also
includes the firmware that reads the sensors and runs or supports the position estimate, a survey
of Wi-Fi measurements in designated test areas beginning with the Engineering Education and
Research Center (EER) and its adjacent outdoor plazas, and a web or mobile application that
displays the tag's position on a campus map.

Several related capabilities fall outside the project's scope. The tag will not use a cellular
connection such as LTE-M or NB-IoT; these require a paid data plan and a second radio that would
increase cost and power consumption, and campus Wi-Fi already covers the target areas. The team
will not survey every building on campus, because each survey requires hours of walking
measurements per floor; instead, testing will be limited to a few defined benchmark areas, and the
survey method will be documented so that additional buildings can be added later. The prototype
enclosure will be 3D-printed or purchased rather than injection-molded for mass production, since
the project's goal is a working proof of concept rather than a manufacturable product. Finally,
BevoTag is designed to locate objects, not people, and features intended to follow a person, such
as continuous covert tracking, are excluded by design.

\subsubsection{Design Challenges}
Three challenges will most strongly shape the design. The first is the trade-off between computing
on the tag and transmitting raw data. Processing CSI and running the EKF on a small
microcontroller strains its memory and battery, whereas sending raw measurements to the server
moves that work off the tag but keeps the radio on longer, which also drains the battery. The team
will settle this trade-off by measuring actual CSI capture rates, IMU data rates, and power draw on
the prototype hardware.

The second challenge is the variability of indoor radio signals. The Wi-Fi measurements recorded
at a given spot change from moment to moment as people walk through hallways, doors open and
close, and network traffic varies \cite{jiang}, so the matching method must tolerate measurements
that never exactly repeat the survey. The third challenge is IMU drift. Low-cost motion sensors
accumulate position error within seconds \cite{mukherjee}, so Wi-Fi fixes must arrive often enough,
and be reliable enough, to keep the EKF from diverging from the true position.

\subsubsection{Relevant Standards}
Four sets of standards constrain how BevoTag uses the radio spectrum, connects its sensors, and
protects its data. Appendix~A lists the specific clauses of each standard and how they affect the
design.

IEEE 802.11 \cite{std80211} defines how Wi-Fi devices format and exchange signals. It
determines which Wi-Fi chips can report CSI, because CSI is derived from the training symbols that
the standard places at the start of every packet, and it governs how the tag connects to the campus
network.

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) regulates radio emissions in the United States under
Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 15 \cite{fcc15}. Part 15 limits both the
intended Wi-Fi transmissions and the unintended electrical noise that the tag's processor and
circuits emit, so that the tag does not interfere with campus communications. Choosing a Wi-Fi
module that already holds FCC certification is the most practical way to meet these limits.

The IEEE 1451 family of standards \cite{std1451} describes how sensors should present their data
and calibration information to a processor. Following its approach keeps the IMU and Wi-Fi
drivers separate from the position-estimation code, so that either sensor can be replaced without
rewriting the filter.

Finally, NIST Special Publication 800-88 \cite{nist80088} and FIPS 140-3 \cite{fips1403} set
federal guidelines for erasing stored data and for the cryptographic methods used to protect it.
Because the tag stores network credentials and location history, these guidelines inform how it
encrypts its reports and how a factory reset erases its memory.

\subsubsection{Ethical Considerations}
A device whose purpose is to report location creates ethical obligations that extend beyond the
team's own users. The team has identified five areas of responsibility: the privacy of bystanders,
the risk of misuse for tracking people, the security of stored location data, the shared campus
network, and the safety and environmental impact of the hardware.

The first obligation concerns people near the tag. To capture CSI, the tag listens to Wi-Fi traffic
that may come from other people's devices. The firmware will therefore record only the signal
measurements and packet header fields needed for positioning, such as the access point address and
signal strength. It will not decode, store, or transmit the contents of anyone's network traffic.

The second obligation is to prevent misuse. Consumer tracking tags have been hidden in other
people's belongings to follow them without consent. Although BevoTag is designed to track objects,
the team must consider how a tag could be misused against a person. Location data will be visible
only to the registered owner of each tag, and the team will evaluate safeguards such as alerting a
person when an unregistered tag appears to be traveling with them.

The third obligation is to protect location data once collected. A history of where an item has
been reveals where its owner has been, so the data are personal information. The team will encrypt
data in transit between the tag and the server, restrict server access to authenticated owners,
retain location history only as long as needed for the project, and ensure that a factory reset
erases stored credentials and history, consistent with the guidelines in Appendix~A.

The fourth obligation is to the campus network that BevoTag depends on. The tag will listen
passively or connect as an ordinary client and will never transmit traffic designed to disrupt,
congest, or probe UT Austin's network. It will use only networks, such as \texttt{utexas-iot}, that
the university provides for devices of this kind \cite{utiot}.

The fifth obligation is the physical safety and environmental impact of the device. The tag will
carry a small lithium battery near people and their belongings, so the design will include
protection against overcharging, short circuits, and overheating. To limit electronic waste, the
team will favor a rechargeable battery over disposable cells and will document how to dispose of
the prototype's battery properly.

\subsubsection{Project Deliverables}
At the end of the project, the team will deliver an integrated hardware and software prototype that
estimates and displays the location of a tagged item within the surveyed campus areas. The
prototype will consist of five deliverables:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BevoTag hardware device:} a compact device with a custom circuit board,
    enclosure, and battery that collects Wi-Fi and motion measurements and sends them to the
    localization system.
  \item \textbf{Embedded firmware:} software on the tag that reads the sensors, timestamps each
    measurement, monitors device status, and communicates over Wi-Fi.
  \item \textbf{Campus map:} a digital 3D map of the designated test areas that contains the
    spatial information needed to display and evaluate the tag's estimated location.
  \item \textbf{Localization backend:} server software that receives data from each tag,
    estimates its position from the Wi-Fi and motion measurements, and provides the result to the
    user application.
  \item \textbf{User application:} a web or mobile application in which a user can view a tag's
    estimated location and status on the campus map.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Requirements Specification}
A successful BevoTag implementation must provide continuous location estimates for a tagged item
across representative indoor and outdoor areas of campus and must present them in a form that a
user can readily interpret. The specifications in this subsection translate the design problem into
measurable terms. They define the system's inputs and outputs, its user interface, the conditions
under which it must operate, and the performance it must achieve, without prescribing a particular
implementation. Wi-Fi and inertial sensing form the primary method; other positioning methods,
such as GNSS outdoors, may be added if they improve performance.

\subsubsection{Input and Output Specifications}
The BevoTag system consists of three components that must work together to produce a location
estimate: the tag, a backend server, and the user application. Figure~\ref{fig:io} shows the inputs
each component receives, the outputs it produces, and the communication paths between them. The
tag measures Wi-Fi signals, motion, and, when available outdoors, GNSS position, and it sends
these measurements and its status to the server. The server estimates the tag's position and
passes it to the application, which displays it to the user. A direct USB-C connection between the
tag and a computer running the application allows configuration and debugging. These paths may
change as the team makes further design decisions.

\subsubsection{Performance Specifications}
Successful operation requires more than producing a location value; the estimates must be accurate,
frequent, and timely enough for BevoTag to serve as a useful tracker. Existing Wi-Fi positioning
systems show that meter-level accuracy is achievable, but performance varies widely with the
sensing method and the radio environment \cite{jiang}. The specifications in Table~\ref{tab:perf}
therefore define performance in terms of repeatable measurements.

The team will evaluate performance at surveyed points whose true positions are known
independently of BevoTag's estimate. Tests will include both stationary and moving trials, indoors
and outdoors, and will be repeated at different times of day to capture the effect of changing
conditions such as pedestrian traffic.

\subsection{Team Policies}
With five members working in parallel on hardware, firmware, positioning algorithms, and the user
application, the team needs shared rules for communicating, tracking work, holding members
accountable, and resolving disagreements. The following policies apply to every member and
sub-team, and the team will revisit them at the end of the semester as it prepares for detailed
design.

\subsubsection{Project Management Tools}
The team uses Discord as its primary communication channel, with separate channels for general
coordination, each sub-team, and meeting logistics so that discussions stay organized and
searchable. Members are expected to answer direct questions within 24 hours on weekdays and to flag
time-sensitive issues, such as a blocked task or an approaching deadline, by mentioning the affected
members directly. The Project Lead coordinates email with Dr.\ Vikalo and the Technical TA and
copies the full team so that every member receives the same guidance.

The team meets twice a week and meets with Dr.\ Vikalo every two weeks. A shared Google Calendar
holds meeting times, course deadlines, and internal milestones. Firmware, application code, data
processing scripts, and circuit board and enclosure designs are kept under version control in a
shared GitHub repository, where GitHub Issues and a GitHub Projects board record each task's owner,
due date, and status. Reports, meeting notes, and survey data are stored in a shared Google Drive
folder.

\subsubsection{Team Expectations}
Each member is expected to attend both weekly team meetings and every mentor meeting, having
reviewed the agenda beforehand, and to notify the team on Discord at least 24 hours in advance of an
unavoidable absence. A member who misses a meeting is responsible for reading the notes and
following up on any action items assigned to them. Internal deadlines fall at least 48 hours before
each course deadline so the team has time to integrate, review, and revise its work.

Every deliverable should meet the standard the team would present to its mentor. Code is reviewed
by at least one other member through a pull request before it is merged, hardware designs are
checked by a second member before boards are ordered, and written sections are proofread by another
member against the ECE Style Guide. At the start of each meeting, every member briefly reports what
they completed, what they plan to do next, and any blockers, and the Project Lead records these
updates and keeps the Projects board current. A member who expects to miss an internal deadline must
tell the team at least 48 hours in advance so the work can be re-scoped or redistributed.

\subsubsection{Conflict Resolution}
The team makes significant decisions, such as selecting a positioning approach, choosing components,
or changing the project scope, by majority vote after the options have been researched. The members
most familiar with the question present the trade-offs of each option, supported by datasheets,
published results, cost estimates, or the team's own measurements, and the outcome and its
rationale are recorded in the meeting notes. Decisions that affect only one sub-team are made within
that sub-team and reported at the next meeting.

Technical disagreements are settled with evidence rather than persistence. Where practical, the
team runs a quick test or trade study against the performance specifications in
Table~\ref{tab:perf} before voting, and it brings any unresolved disagreement that poses significant
risk to Dr.\ Vikalo or the Technical TA. Interpersonal conflicts are first addressed privately
between the members involved; if that does not resolve the issue, an uninvolved member mediates, and
the team escalates any conflict it cannot resolve to the Technical TA or the course instructor.

When a member does not meet a responsibility, the team responds in escalating steps. A first missed
commitment is raised privately to understand the cause and agree on a recovery plan. Repeated missed
deadlines or meetings are discussed at a team meeting, and corrective actions are recorded in the
notes. If the problem continues, the team will notify the Technical TA and the course instructor and
will report each member's contributions accurately in peer evaluations.

\section{Conclusion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This Project Definition documents the team's understanding of the BevoTag design problem and how
the team has organized itself to solve it. Satellite positioning fails inside campus buildings, and
existing trackers depend on closed networks of other people's phones, so the team proposes a
low-cost tag that locates itself by matching campus Wi-Fi measurements against a building survey and
fusing them with motion-sensor data. The report defined the functions, scope, standards, and ethical
obligations of that design, set measurable requirements including a median error under 5~m and room
identification above 90\%, and assigned roles and working policies to the team.

The project carries real risk: CSI must be captured on low-power hardware, room-level accuracy
depends on careful building surveys, and campus network coverage and access policies may limit where
the tag can operate. Even so, the team's combined experience in embedded systems, positioning, and
software gives it a realistic path to a working prototype within a set of surveyed buildings. Next,
the team will revise this document using feedback from the Technical TA and the Writing
Consultation, complete a data logger for initial Wi-Fi and IMU measurements, survey EER as the first
test building, and refine the system block diagram and component selection.
